01 · Overview

Workflow

Mill green, seal the ends the same hour, give every slab an ID, stack it on stickers under a roof with open sides, let the air bring it to about 20%, then finish in a solar kiln to 7–9%. Everything that happens to a slab is logged against its ID, with photos.

  1. Before the first cut. Written agreement with the landowner and between partners (ch. 16). Species identified (ch. 12). Yard, roof, stickers and slab-moving equipment ready.
  2. Mill. Oversize the thickness, keep slabs in order, one crew member on logging and sealing.
  3. Within the hour: seal ends, mark the ID, nail the tag, photograph, weigh sample slabs, cut a moisture wafer per log.
  4. Move and stack on level foundations, stickers every 16″, weighted top, roof overhang, open sides.
  5. Log moisture every 2 weeks for the first 2 months, then monthly. Weight-based MC for thick slabs.
  6. Kiln at 20–25% (thin stock first), equalize, condition, heat-sterilize (133°F core, 30 min).
  7. Finish, grade, photograph, price, then sell locally or load a container.
Thickness sets the calendar

A 6-month air-dry plan suits 2″ slabs and gets 4″ close for easy species. 6″ slabs take a year or more. Chapter 10 and the drying estimator in Calculators have the numbers.

02 · Equipment

Big-bar mill

An Alaskan-style mill is two bar clamps joined by two rails. To cut logs over about 60″ you need a 72–84″ bar and a frame to match: buy a large mill, or lengthen and reinforce a smaller one. This chapter covers converting a 48″ mill to take a 76″ bar.

Usable width = bar length minus what the two clamps and the nose/sprocket take up (typically 8–12″ total). Measure your own rig and write the number on the rail.

Lengthening a 48″ mill

  • Rails: replace the stock rails with longer ones cut to the 76″ bar plus the clamp spacing. Use 2″×1″ rectangular tube (steel, 1/8″ wall, or 6061 aluminum, 3/16″ wall), taller side vertical so it resists sag.
  • Joints: re-drill the stock end brackets to the new rails, or weld/bolt sleeves over the old rail ends with at least 6″ of overlap and two bolts each side.
  • Squareness: add two diagonal braces between the rails (in plan view) so the frame can't rack into a parallelogram. Racking tilts the bar and makes wedge-shaped slabs.
  • Stiffener: where the rails overhang the log on narrow cuts, a light king-post truss (above) or a second rail stacked on top keeps them straight.
  • Clamps: check that the end clamps grip the bar without twisting it. Add a second clamp bolt at the nose end if it creeps.
  • Weight: every pound you add is a pound someone pushes for hours. Aluminum rails, steel only where it clamps.

Roller conveyor mod

  • Swap sliding contact for gravity conveyor rollers (1.9″ Ø, steel or PVC) or skate wheels on axles bolted under the rails. Rolling cuts push force noticeably, especially on sticky green sap.
  • Mount 2–3 rollers per rail, axles parallel to the rails so they roll along the log.
  • Rollers raise the frame. Re-zero the thickness scale: measure bar-to-roller-bottom with a square and recalibrate the posts.
  • Keep the guide surface swept. Sawdust windrows lift a roller and put a bump in the slab.
  • No rollers? Screw UHMW polyethylene strips under the rails. Cheap, slick, and they don't change height much.

Bar, chain, oil

  • Ripping chain (10° top-plate angle), .404″ pitch, .063″ gauge on bars over 60″. Skip or semi-skip clears long chips better.
  • Auxiliary oiler feeding the nose end. The saw's pump can't oil a 76″ bar alone. With two heads, both pumps run plus the aux oiler.
  • Double-ended bars (Granberg, Cannon, others) take a powerhead at each end. On a 76–84″ bar that's the single biggest speed-up you can buy.
  • 3–4 sharpened loops on hand. Touch up every tank.
03 · Feed

04 · Procedure

Milling day

  1. Clean the log. Strip bark on the cut line; sand and rocks dull chain in seconds. Sweep with a metal detector for nails, wire and old hardware.
  2. Stabilize. Log off the ground on cribbing, wedged so it can't roll. Knee-to-thigh height saves backs.
  3. First cut on a guide. Screw two straight 2×10s, an aluminum ladder, or a slabbing rail across the top, shimmed level and in one plane. It sets every slab below it.
  4. Set thickness, cut, wedge. Each later cut rides on the last face. Drive plastic wedges into the kerf behind the bar.
  5. Lift off with equipment (chapter 05). Big green slabs are 500–1,500 lb.
  6. Keep sequence. Bookmatch sets sell for more. Mark the ID before the slab leaves the log.

The pith slab

The slab through the center of the tree is most likely to split. Cut it thicker and plan bowties, rip it down the pith into two narrower slabs, or cut two slabs whose faces sit just either side of it. Many big tropical trunks are hollow or punky in the middle: look at the end grain first.

Slab rail build (overhead monorail)

Parts

  • Beam: steel I-beam (W6 or S6 or larger) sized by an engineer or a steel yard's span table for your span and load. Don't guess beam sizes.
  • Posts: steel or 6×6/8×8 timbers set in concrete, knee-braced to the beam in both directions. Or use two A-frame gantry cranes as the end supports.
  • Beam trolley (push type) matched to the beam flange width.
  • Chain hoist 1–2 ton (manual or electric).
  • Spreader bar 6–8′ with two lifting points, plus two web slings (nylon, not chain, so they don't bite the slab).
  • End stops bolted at both beam ends.

Rules

  • Every component's working load limit (WLL) stamped and above your heaviest slab. The weakest part sets the limit.
  • Nobody under the load. Guide it with a tag line or a hand on the end, not a body under it.
  • Lift a few inches, pause, check balance, then travel.
  • Travel low, over clear ground.
  • Inspect slings, hooks and chain before each session.
06 · Same hour

Seal the ends

End grain loses water 10–15 times faster than face grain. Unsealed ends shrink and split back into the slab, often a foot or more. Sealing is the cheapest step with the biggest payoff.

  • Seal within an hour of cutting. Also seal log ends as soon as you buck.
  • Wax emulsion end sealer (Anchorseal 2 or equivalent), thick coat over the whole end, the first inch of the faces and the bark edge.
  • Seal large checks, knot holes and exposed pith on faces.
  • Recoat anything that flakes or gets trimmed. Roughly 100–150 ft² of end grain per gallon; buy 5-gallon pails.
  • Do not seal the faces while drying.

08 · Air drying

Air-drying yard

Steady, gentle drying: off the rain, out of direct sun, air moving through every layer.

Foundation

  • Bottom layer 12–18″ off the ground on blocks or treated bearers every 2′ or less.
  • All bearers level and in one plane. A twisted foundation dries a twisted slab.
  • Weeds cut, gravel or weed cloth, no standing water.

Stickers

  • Dry, ¾–1″ thick, 1½″ wide, all the same thickness.
  • Every 16″ (12″ for warp-prone species like kamani, sycamore, elm), one flush with each end.
  • Stacked directly above each other in columns down to the bearers.
  • Wet or hardwood stickers on pale sapwood leave sticker stain.

Stack

  • One thickness per stack or layer, thick slabs at the bottom.
  • 1–2″ gaps side by side, slabs in sequence.
  • Weight the top: 50–100 lb per ft², or ratchet straps.
  • Stacks 6′+ apart, long side to the prevailing wind.

Roof, not walls

  • 1–2′ roof overhang all round. Sides open.
  • No tarps draped over stacks: they trap moisture and grow mold.
  • Shade cloth on the sunny side for the first months.
First 4 weeks are the riskiest

Walk the stacks weekly. Mold: more spacing and airflow. Surface checks: more shade, less wind. Log problems with a photo.

Air drying has a floor

Wood outdoors only dries to the local equilibrium moisture content (EMC): usually 12–18% depending on climate. The kiln takes it the rest of the way. Dry slabs stored outside pick moisture back up.

09 · Measurement

Measuring moisture

Moisture content (MC) = weight of water ÷ weight of the dry wood. Green wood can be 60–150%. Target is 7–9% for most indoor markets (6–8% for heated northern interiors).

1 · Oven-dry wafer (the reference)

  1. Cut a 1″ wafer across a slab at least 12″ from the end. One per log minimum.
  2. Remove splinters and bark; weigh at once on a 0.1 g scale (wet weight).
  3. Oven at 215–220°F (101–105°C), reweigh until the weight stops dropping (oven-dry weight).
  4. MC % = (wet − oven-dry) ÷ oven-dry × 100

2 · Sample slabs by weight (how you track thick stock)

Pick 3–5 representative sample slabs per thickness and species. Weigh each green with a crane scale; use the wafer MC from the same log to work out its bone-dry weight. Every later weighing gives its MC:

Oven-dry weight = green weight ÷ (1 + green MC/100)Current MC % = (current weight ÷ oven-dry weight − 1) × 100

Example: 610 lb green, wafer 105% → oven-dry 297.6 lb. Later 372 lb → MC 25%. The Slab log does this math automatically.

11 · Finish drying

Solar kiln

The Virginia Tech solar kiln is the proven low-cost design: a sloped clear roof over a black absorber, insulated walls, fans pushing hot air through the stack, and vents to exhaust moist air. Free plans are linked in Gear & build plans.

Sizing

  • VT rule: 1 ft² of clear roof per 10 board feet of 1″ lumber. Plans exist for 800–1,000 bf and 1,500–2,000 bf.
  • For 2″+ slabs that ratio gives more heat than needed: load more, or shade part of the roof early.
  • Inside width = widest slab + 12″ air space each side (66″ slabs → 8′+ inside).
  • Inside length = longest slab + 2–3′ for fans and baffle.

Build

  • Twin-wall UV polycarbonate roof, sloped near your latitude, facing the equator.
  • Black-painted corrugated metal absorber under the glazing.
  • Insulated walls and floor (R-10+), sealed inside.
  • 2–4 fans across the absorber and down through the stack; a baffle forces air through the sticker gaps.
  • Intake vent low one end, exhaust high the other, with flaps.
  • Fork-width door. Thermostat so fans run only when warm (or solar fans). WiFi temp/RH logger.

Loading

  • Same sticker rules as the yard; sample slabs reachable.
  • One thickness per charge where possible.
  • Weight or strap the top. Fill the space so air can't bypass.

Heat-sterilize

133°F (56°C) core for 30 minutes kills insects and eggs (the ISPM-15 standard). Probe thermometer in a hole drilled to the center of a thick sample slab; log the time held. Buyers and inspectors ask.

Prong test

Cut a 1″ wafer, saw out the middle to leave two prongs, leave it overnight. Prongs pinch in: condition longer. Straight: done.

12 · Identification

Species ID & legality

Species drives price, drying schedule, safety and legality. Identify the tree while it's still standing or on the ground, when leaves, pods and bark are available, and keep the evidence with the tree record.

  1. Collect: leaves (both sides), flowers or pods, a bark photo, and the whole tree's form.
  2. End grain: slice a clean surface with a razor and look with a 10× hand lens: pore size and arrangement, rays, tyloses.
  3. Density: weigh and measure a dry block (lb/ft³) or note if it floats.
  4. Scent of fresh shavings, and fluorescence: shine a 365 nm UV flashlight on fresh-cut heartwood, and on shavings soaked in water.
  5. Confirm valuable or regulated wood with an expert: the USDA Forest Products Lab's Center for Wood Anatomy Research, a university extension, or the InsideWood database.

"Burmese rosewood": which one is it?

The name is used for two very different woods. Most "Burmese rosewood" growing as a street or park tree (Hawaii, Florida, SE Asia) is narra, Pterocarpus indicus. The same name is used in the timber trade for true rosewoods, Dalbergia oliveri / bariensis, which are CITES-regulated.

Regulated wood

CITES governs cross-border trade: listed species (all Dalbergia, big-leaf mahogany, several Pterocarpus) need permits to export or import. The US Lacey Act makes it illegal to trade wood taken in violation of any law, and requires a declaration for many imports. For domestically grown trees, keep the tree record (owner, location, date, photos, ID evidence). It's your proof of legal origin.

13 · After drying

Flatten & seal

Sell rough (cheapest), flattened and sanded (best value per hour), or finished (highest price and labor). Most slab yards sell flattened and sanded.

Flatten

  • Router sled with a 2–2½″ slab-flattening bit. Shim so the slab can't rock; flatten one face, flip, do the other.
  • Or pay a shop with a wide-belt sander or CNC; price per ft² first.
  • Sand 80 → 120 → 180; raise the grain and re-sand on fuzzy species.
  • Wire-brush or drawknife the bark edge.

Cracks and voids

  • Bowties across cracks, after drying. Router template plus chisel.
  • Voids: slow-cure or deep-pour epoxy, clear or tinted. Tape the back of through-cracks.
  • Stabilize punky or spalted spots with thin penetrating epoxy.

Sealing for sale

  • Unfinished slabs: one coat of dewaxed shellac or sanding sealer slows moisture pickup in transit.
  • Finished: hardwax oil (Rubio Monocoat, Osmo Polyx) or catalyzed / water-based poly for hard use.
  • Oily woods (rosewoods, teak): wipe with acetone before finish or glue.
  • Coat both faces equally or the slab can cup.
  • Store dry slabs indoors or shrink-wrapped.

Price from real comparables

Before anything is listed or shipped, collect 10 current listings per species in each target market (slab yards, Marketplace, Etsy, Craigslist). Record width, length, thickness, finish and price, and compute $/bf. Rare, wide, dry and flat is what earns the top of the range.

Listing checklist

  • Full-slab photo of both faces in daylight, one wiped with mineral spirits to show color, close-ups of figure and defects.
  • ID, species (common and scientific name), thickness, length, width at both ends and middle, board feet.
  • Final MC with date, kiln-dried and heat-treated (133°F core).
  • Finish state and any fills or bowties.
  • Pickup location, freight options, and origin documentation for regulated species.
15 · Logistics

Container to market

A container pays off only with dry, sorted, photographed slabs that are mostly pre-sold or listed. Green wood in a steel box arrives covered in mold.

Road limits usually cap a 40′ at roughly 42,000–44,000 lb of cargo, so heavy species weigh out before the box is full. Light species (earpod ~27 lb/ft³) fill the space first. Use the load planner in Calculators and confirm with your forwarder.

Packing

  • Only slabs at target MC, each metered and logged with stage "Shipped".
  • Bundle by grade on 4×4 skids with dry stickers, strapped, so forks can unload them.
  • Stretch-wrap or blanket; protect edges and corners.
  • Container desiccant on the walls: boxes condense ("container rain") crossing temperature zones.
  • Block and brace; heavy bundles low and centered.
  • Packing list per box from the Slab log export: ID, species, size, bf, grade, photo.

Paperwork

  • Islands and territories (e.g. Hawaii): mainland ocean freight is limited to Jones Act carriers. Call USDA APHIS Plant Protection and Quarantine before booking; keep your heat-treatment log.
  • International: phytosanitary certificate, ISPM-15 for any wood packaging, CITES permits for listed species, Lacey Act declaration on US imports.
  • Domestic: check state quarantines (walnut thousand cankers, oak wilt, invasive borers) for logs; kiln-dried, bark-free wood is usually exempt.
  • Three door-to-door quotes including drayage both ends; ask about demurrage and per-diem. Insure at declared value.

16 · Agreements

Partners & landowners

Trees, labor, equipment and a year or more of drying: write the deal down before milling day and have everyone sign.

With the landowner

  • Who owns the slabs: the miller, the owner, or a split (slabs of their choice or a % of sales).
  • Milling fee, tree removal and hauling costs.
  • Site access, how long logs can stay, cleanup.
  • Liability and insurance on site.
  • Written proof of ownership and permission to harvest (your legal-origin record).

Between partners

  • Who puts in cash and who puts in labor or equipment.
  • Costs reimbursed first, then profit split (equal or weighted).
  • Who sets prices and accepts offers; the floor price anyone can accept.
  • What happens if someone leaves, or stock doesn't sell.
  • One ledger for every expense and sale, with receipt photos.

A planning checklist, not legal or tax advice. An hour with a local attorney or accountant is worth it at these amounts. The split calculator is in Calculators.
