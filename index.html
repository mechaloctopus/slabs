01 · Overview

The plan

Mill the tree green, seal the ends the same day, tag every slab, stack it on stickers under a roof with open sides, and let the air take it down to about 20% moisture. Then finish in a solar kiln to 7–9%, flatten or at least surface the best pieces, and sell. Everything you do is recorded against the slab's ID.

  1. Before the cut. Written agreement with the tree owners and between the three of you (section 15). Yard, roof and stickers ready before a single slab comes off the log.
  2. Mill. Cut 2″, 4″ and 6″ slabs oversize. Keep every slab in its original order.
  3. Within the hour: seal both ends, write the ID on the slab, nail the tag, photograph it, weigh it if you can, and cut a moisture wafer from the first slab of each log.
  4. Stack on level foundations, stickers every 16″, weighted top, roof overhang, open sides.
  5. Log moisture every 2 weeks for the first 2 months, then monthly. Weight-based numbers beat meter numbers on thick slabs.
  6. Kiln when the sample slabs are at or under 20–25%. Thin stock first, thick stock on a gentler schedule.
  7. Equalize, condition, sterilize (core at 133°F for 30 min), then store dry and covered. Wrap before shipping.
  8. Sell with a photo, ID, dimensions, board feet and final MC for every slab.
Reality check on the 6-month plan

Six months under cover works for the 2″ slabs and gets 4″ slabs close. It does not dry 6″ slabs. Plan on 12–18 months total for the 6″ stock. Section 09 has the numbers.

02 · Species

03 · Equipment

Build the mill

A 78″ bar cutting a 66″ log leaves very little spare. The mill clamps eat into the bar at both ends, so plan on roughly 70″ of usable cut width. Measure yours once it's built and write the real number on the frame.

Powerhead

  • Use the biggest saw you can get: 120cc class (Stihl MS 881, Husqvarna 3120XP) or equivalent.
  • On a bar this long, a second powerhead on the nose end (double-ended mill) doubles power and halves the time per cut. It's worth considering for a job with hundreds of cuts.
  • Rich enough mix, clean air filter every tank. Milling is full throttle for minutes at a time.

Bar, chain, oil

  • Ripping chain (10° top-plate angle, not the 30° crosscut angle). .404″ pitch is the usual choice on long bars.
  • Skip or semi-skip chain clears the long chips better on a 78″ bar.
  • Auxiliary oiler feeding the nose. The saw's pump can't oil 78″ of bar by itself.
  • Touch up the chain every tank. Keep 3–4 sharpened loops so you swap and keep cutting.

Fabricating the frame

  • Crossmember across the full bar length has to be stiff. 2″×1″ or 2″×2″ rectangular steel tube, not square aluminum, or the middle of the bar sags and your slabs come out thick in the center.
  • Two bar clamps (powerhead end and nose end), each with a height post that adjusts in ¼″ steps and locks positively.
  • Rollers or skids on the rails so the mill glides on the guide.
  • Helper handle on the nose end. One person pushes, one pulls and keeps the nose tracking.
  • Buying instead: the Granberg G777 Alaskan (MK-IV) takes bars up to 84″ (listed in the gear list).
Flare and bark

The butt of a big earpod usually flares well past its trunk diameter. If the flare is wider than your 70″, cut the flare off with a crosscut first, or slab past it and accept that the first few feet of those slabs won't fit.

04 · Procedure

Milling day

  1. Clean the log. Wire-brush or strip bark along the cut line. Sand and embedded rocks kill a chain in seconds. Check for nails, fence wire and old tree hardware with a metal detector.
  2. Get it up and stable. Log off the dirt on cribbing or blocks, wedged so it cannot roll. Working height around knee to thigh saves your back.
  3. First cut on a guide. Screw a pair of straight 2×8 or 2×10 boards, an aluminum ladder, or a purpose-built slabbing rail to the top of the log. Shim it level and in one plane. That first flat face sets every slab under it.
  4. Set thickness, then cut. Every later cut rides on the previous face. Drive plastic wedges into the kerf behind the bar so the slab can't pinch it.
  5. Lift slabs off with a plan. A green 66″ × 10′ × 4″ earpod slab can weigh 700–900 lb. Use a tractor, skid steer with forks, a gantry and chain hoist, or rollers and a ramp. Don't hand-carry the big ones.
  6. Keep them in order. Stack sequence slabs together (bookmatch sets sell for more). Mark the ID before the slab leaves the log.

The pith slab

The slab through the center of the tree (the pith) is the one most likely to split while drying. Options: cut it thicker and accept a crack you'll bowtie later, rip it down the pith into two narrower slabs, or cut two slabs whose faces sit just either side of the pith. Big earpod trunks are often hollow or punky in the middle anyway, so check the end grain first.

05 · Same day

Seal the ends

End grain loses water 10–15 times faster than face grain. Unsealed ends dry, shrink, and split back into the slab, sometimes a foot or more. Sealing is the cheapest thing you'll do and it saves the most money.

  • Seal within an hour of the cut, ideally as soon as the slab comes off the log. Also seal the fresh log ends when you buck it.
  • Use a wax emulsion end sealer such as Anchorseal 2. Brush or roll a thick coat across the whole end, including the first inch of the faces and the bark edge.
  • Seal any big check, knot hole or exposed pith on the face too.
  • Recoat anything that flakes or that you trim later. A gallon covers roughly 100–150 ft² of end grain; buy it by the 5-gallon pail.
  • Do not seal the faces. The slab needs to dry through them.
06 · Inventory

Label every slab

With hundreds of slabs from several trees, the ID is the only thing that ties a slab to its moisture history, its photos and its price. Use one format and never reuse an ID.

EP01-A-072.5″ · 10′2″ · milled 2026-10-12
EP01-A-082.5″ · bookmatch of -07
EP

Species code. EP earpod, MK monkeypod, MG mango, etc.

01

Tree number. Keep a tree list: owner, location, date felled, photos.

A

Log letter. A is the butt log, then B, C going up the tree.

07

Slab number counting from the first cut (01 is the top cap slab).

Mark it three ways

  1. Aluminum tag on one end, stamped with a letter/number punch set, fixed with aluminum nails only. Steel staples or nails ruin planer knives and saw teeth later.
  2. Paint marker or lumber crayon on the face near both ends. Rain and sealer can hide the tag; the face mark is your backup.
  3. Photo of the full slab with the tag in the shot, the day it's milled. Name the file with the ID. Later add a dry photo and a finished photo.

Optional: print weatherproof QR labels that link to the slab's row in your shared spreadsheet. Buyers like scanning it at a showroom.

07 · Air drying

Air-drying yard

Goal: steady, gentle drying. Keep off the rain, keep out of direct sun, keep air moving through every layer.

Foundation

  • Bottom layer at least 12–18″ off the ground. Concrete blocks or treated 4×6 / 6×6 bearers every 2′ or less, so a 10′ slab sits on 5–6 supports.
  • All bearers level and in one plane (string line or laser). A twisted foundation dries a twisted slab.
  • Weeds cut, ground covered with gravel or weed cloth, no standing water.

Stickers

  • Dry stickers, ¾–1″ thick, 1½″ wide, all exactly the same thickness. Use pine, fir or dry earpod offcuts.
  • Every 16″ along the slab (12″ for 2″ stock if it wants to warp), one flush with each end so the ends can't curl.
  • Stickers stacked directly above each other in vertical columns, all the way down to the bearers.
  • Wet stickers or hardwood stickers on sapwood leave sticker stain. Keep them dry.

The stack

  • One thickness per stack or per layer. Put the thick slabs at the bottom.
  • Slabs in sequence, live edges not touching, 1–2″ gaps side by side for airflow.
  • Weight the top: concrete blocks or ratchet straps, around 50–100 lb per ft² of top area. Keeps the top layers from cupping.
  • Stacks 6′ apart or more, long side facing the prevailing wind.

Roof, not walls

  • Roof with 1–2′ overhang on all sides. Metal roofing on a frame, or a simple carport.
  • Sides open. Tarps draped over a stack trap moisture and grow mold. If you tarp, tarp the roof only.
  • Shade cloth on the sunny side in the first months. Direct sun on green thick slabs checks the surface.
First 4 weeks are the riskiest

Walk the stacks weekly. Look for surface checks, mold, and stain. Mold on the surface: more airflow and spacing. Surface checks on the top layers: more shade, less wind. Photograph problems with the tag visible and note them in the journal.

Air drying has a floor

Outdoor wood only dries to the local equilibrium moisture content (EMC). In a humid, warm climate that's usually 13–18%. You can't air dry to 8%; that's what the kiln is for. And kiln-dried slabs stored back outside will pick moisture back up.

08 · Measurement

Measuring moisture

Moisture content (MC) is the weight of water divided by the weight of the dry wood. Green earpod can start near or above 100%. You're taking it down to 7–9%. Three tools, from most to least trustworthy:

1 · Oven-dry wafer (the truth)

  1. When milling, cut a 1″-thick wafer across the slab at least 12″ from the end (the end has already dried). One per log is the minimum, one per thickness is better.
  2. Knock off splinters and bark. Weigh it immediately on a gram scale (0.1 g). That's the wet weight.
  3. Dry it in an oven at 215–220°F (101–105°C). Reweigh every few hours until the weight stops dropping. That's the oven-dry weight.
  4. MC % = (wet − oven-dry) ÷ oven-dry × 100

2 · Weighing sample slabs (how you track thick stock)

Pick 3–5 representative sample slabs per thickness and make them easy to get at in the stack. Weigh each one green with a hanging crane scale, and use the wafer MC from the same log to work out what it will weigh when bone dry. After that, every weighing gives you its MC:

Oven-dry weight = green weight ÷ (1 + green MC/100)Current MC % = (current weight ÷ oven-dry weight − 1) × 100

Example: 610 lb green, wafer says 105% MC. Oven-dry weight = 610 ÷ 2.05 = 297.6 lb. Two months later it weighs 372 lb, so MC = (372 ÷ 297.6 − 1) × 100 = 25%. The journal (section 11) does this math for you.

The US Forest Products Lab uses this sample-board method to run commercial kilns. It averages the whole slab, core included, which no meter can do on a 4–6″ slab.

10 · Finish drying

Solar kiln

Build to the Virginia Tech solar kiln design and adjust the size for your slabs. It's a proven, cheap design: a sloped clear roof over a black absorber, insulated walls, fans that push hot air through the stack, and vents to dump the moist air.

Sizing

  • Rule of the VT design: 1 ft² of clear roof per 10 board feet of 1″ lumber. VT plans come in 800–1,000 bf and 1,500–2,000 bf versions.
  • For thick slabs, this ratio gives more heat than needed, so you can load more bf per ft² of glazing, or shade part of the roof early on.
  • Inside width must take your widest slab plus 12″+ of air space on each side. For 60–66″ slabs that means at least 8′ inside.
  • Inside length = longest slab + 2–3′ for fan and baffle space.

Build

  • Roof glazing: twin-wall polycarbonate (UV-rated), sloped close to your latitude, facing the equator.
  • Absorber under the glazing: corrugated metal painted flat black.
  • Walls and floor insulated (foam board, R-10 or better), interior sealed with a vapor barrier or paint.
  • 2–4 circulation fans pushing air across the absorber and down through the stack. A baffle forces air through the stickered layers instead of over the top.
  • Two vents with flaps: intake low on one end, exhaust high on the other.
  • Big door on one end so you can load with forks.
  • A thermostat switch so fans run only when the kiln is warm (e.g., an Inkbird controller in cooling mode, or solar-powered fans that run with the sun).
  • A WiFi temperature/humidity logger inside so you can check without opening the door.

Loading

  • Same sticker rules as the yard. Sample slabs where you can reach them.
  • Same thickness in one charge, or thick on the bottom and pulled out later.
  • Weight or strap the top layer.
  • Fill the kiln. Empty space lets air bypass the stack.

Operating by moisture content

Sterilize before you ship

Wood held at a core temperature of 133°F (56°C) for 30 minutes kills insects and eggs. That's the ISPM-15 heat-treat standard. Drill a hole to the center of a thick sample slab, insert a probe thermometer, seal around it, and log the time the core holds 133°F+. Write it in the journal. Buyers and freight inspectors ask.

Case-hardening check (prong test)

Crosscut a 1″ wafer from a dry test piece, cut out the center to leave a U with two prongs, and leave it overnight. Prongs pinch inward: still stressed, condition longer. Prongs stay straight: done.

12 · After drying

Flatten & seal

Decide per slab: sell rough (cheapest, buyer finishes), sell flattened and sanded (best value per hour of work), or sell finished (highest price, highest labor). Most slab yards sell flattened and sanded, unfinished.

Flatten

  • Router sled with a 2–2½″ spoilboard/slab-flattening bit. Shim the slab so it can't rock; flatten one face, flip, flatten the other.
  • Or pay a shop with a wide-belt sander or CNC per slab. Get a price per square foot before you commit.
  • Sand 80 → 120 → 180. Earpod's tension wood fuzzes; a light raise-the-grain wipe and re-sand at 180 helps.
  • Clean the bark edge with a wire brush or drawknife. Loose bark falls off in transit anyway.

Cracks and voids

  • Bowties (butterfly keys) across cracks, installed only after the slab is dry. Router template plus a chisel; contrasting wood sells.
  • Fill voids with slow-cure or deep-pour epoxy, tinted black or clear. Tape the back of through-cracks.
  • Stabilize punky spots with thin penetrating epoxy.

Sealing for sale

  • Unfinished slabs: one coat of dewaxed shellac or a light sanding sealer slows moisture pickup in transit and shows the color. Tell buyers what's on it.
  • Finished slabs: hardwax oils (Rubio Monocoat, Osmo Polyx) are one-day finishes buyers like. Tables that get heavy use: a catalyzed or water-based polyurethane.
  • Finish both faces equally, or the slab can cup.
  • Dry slabs stored outdoors regain moisture. Keep them in the kiln, an enclosed shed, or shrink-wrapped until they ship.

14 · Logistics

Container to market

A full container only makes sense when the slabs are dry, sorted, photographed, and mostly pre-sold or listed. Ship green or half-dry wood in a closed steel box and you'll open it to a container of mold.

Road weight limits usually cap a 40′ box at about 42,000–44,000 lb of cargo; dry earpod will fill the space before it hits that. Confirm with your forwarder.

Packing

  • Only slabs at target MC, verified with a meter on every slab going in. Record the reading in the journal with stage "Shipped".
  • Bundle by grade and size on 4×4 skids with dry stickers, strapped, so they can be forklifted at the other end.
  • Stretch-wrap or cover with moving blankets under the straps. Protect live edges and corners.
  • Container desiccant strips on the walls. Ocean containers "rain" from condensation when they cross temperature zones.
  • Block and brace so nothing can shift. Load heavy bundles low and centered.
  • Packing list: every ID in the box with dimensions, bf, grade, photo link. That list is also your sales sheet.

Paperwork and rules

  • If you're in Hawaii or another island: ocean freight to the mainland is by Jones Act carriers only (Matson, Pasha). Call the USDA APHIS Plant Protection and Quarantine office before you book and ask what they need for kiln-dried hardwood lumber going to the mainland. Keep your heat-treatment log.
  • On the mainland: a container on a truck or rail (or a flatbed with tarps) is a normal domestic freight move. Get quotes from a freight broker.
  • Insure the load at declared value.
  • Get at least three quotes, door to door, including drayage at both ends and what happens if the box sits (demurrage and per-diem charges add up fast).

15 · Partnership

The 3-way deal

Three partners, a tree owner, a year or more of drying, and a container of wood worth real money. Write it down before milling day, and have each person sign.

With the tree owners

  • Who owns the slabs: you, them, or a split (e.g., they keep 2 slabs of their choice, or a % of sales).
  • Milling fee, if any, and who pays for tree removal and hauling.
  • Access to their property, how long logs can sit there, cleanup.
  • Liability for injuries and damage on site.

Between the three of you

  • Who puts in money (bar, chain, saw, kiln, roof, fuel, shipping) and who puts in labor.
  • How costs are paid back: usually all receipts reimbursed first, then profit split.
  • Profit split: equal thirds, or weighted by money and hours.
  • Who can set prices and accept offers, and the lowest price anyone can take without asking.
  • What happens if someone leaves, or if the container doesn't sell.
  • One shared spreadsheet or ledger for every expense and sale, with receipt photos.

Order used: tree owner's share off the top, then shared costs paid from sales (freight, warehouse, commissions), then each partner reimbursed for what they paid, then the rest in equal thirds. Example numbers only.

This is a planning checklist, not legal or tax advice. For the amounts involved, an hour with a local attorney or accountant to set up the agreement is money well spent.
